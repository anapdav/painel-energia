## 2. Where I agree with you — and would go further

### 2.1 European gas is the underappreciated risk
> "Your report flags low European gas storage almost in passing, in the Russia wildcard. I think it deserves more weight. Storage is at **55.9% as of 27 July, against an eight-year average of 73.4% for that date** — the lowest for the date since 2021, and 2021 was the run-up to the 2022 crisis.
>
> The nuance is that the **injection rate is perfectly normal** — about 3,060 GWh/day over June–July, right in the middle of the five-year range. So this isn't a flow problem, it's a starting-point problem. Europe simply began the season too empty.
>
> **My question:** if Europe has to accelerate LNG procurement into Q4 to close that gap, doesn't your Wildcard #2 stop being independent of #1? You'd have Europe bidding against Asia for the same cargoes precisely when Hormuz flows are impaired. Do you see those two as correlated or as separate tail events?"

### 2.2 The product/crude divergence supports your crack thesis
> "Your point on gasoil being tight globally checks out on the US data: distillates are at **109.6 Mb, about 10% below the five-year average**, while crude ex-SPR is only **5.5% below**. So products are meaningfully tighter than crude — which is exactly the asymmetry that justifies your call for wholesale products to outrun crude in the extreme scenario."

## 3. Where my data diverges — the core question

> "Here's where I'd push back, or at least ask you to help me reconcile.
>
> Your $200 path runs through a **sustained 7–8 Mb/d global deficit**, taking ex-China stocks from 94 days to under 70 by Q1'27. But the EIA's own balance shows the deficit at **−5.1 Mb/d in Q2'26** — roughly 30% below your assumption — and **July already at −2.3 Mb/d**. It peaked at −6.3 in May and has been narrowing every month since.
>
> **Three things I'd like to understand:**
> 1. Is the gap methodological — ex-China versus global, crude versus total liquids, treatment of oil on water — or is it a genuine disagreement with the EIA balance?
> 2. What brings the deficit **back** to 7–8? From where I sit, that requires a *new* physical disruption, not a continuation of the current state.
> 3. On observable stocks: OECD commercial inventories fell from 2,802 to 2,590 Mb between January and July — real, but only about 1.2 Mb/d of visible draw against a much larger implied global deficit. Are those missing barrels going into **Chinese** storage? Because if so, that actually makes your ex-China argument *stronger*, not weaker — and I'd want to hear you make that case."

## 4. What I'd add to your framework

### 4.1 The SPR did more of the absorbing than the report credits
> "Something that stood out in the weekly data: **the SPR has drawn 102 million barrels this year** — from 413 Mb in January to 311 Mb now, running at 5 to 10 Mb a week since May. That's roughly 1.2 Mb/d of supply coming out of the US strategic reserve.
>
> So the shock absorber wasn't only shale and commercial stocks — it was substantially a policy release. Two implications: first, part of the 'resilience' the market is celebrating is **borrowed**; second, at 43% of the historical peak, the **ammunition for the next round is materially smaller** than it was in 2022. Does your scenario assume further SPR releases, or is that upside you're deliberately leaving out?"

### 4.2 The supply response has started — which reinforces your timing
> "On the rig count: the trough was **407 in April** and we're at **450 now — up about 11%**. So the drilling response did begin, roughly three months ago. With the standard six-to-nine-month lag, those barrels arrive somewhere between **Q4'26 and mid-2027**.
>
> That's the tension in your timeline: your stock-depletion path bottoms out in Q1'27, which is precisely when the shale response would start landing. Does the supply response arrive in time to blunt the tail, or is it too small relative to a 5–10 Mb/d loss?"

### 4.3 Demand rationing may already be starting
> "US product supplied is running at **20.38 Mb/d on a four-week basis, against 20.56 last year and 20.60 in 2024** — down about 1% year over year. Small, but it's the direction your extreme scenario requires. Are you seeing the same in the OECD demand data?"

## 5. Brazil angle — the gap in the report

> "Two things where I think Brazil deserves more space than the report gives it.
>
> **On Wildcard #9 — the Americas blockade.** You put Brazil in the table at 4.0 Mb/d, but you don't develop it. Our own regulator's data has Brazilian output at a record **4.30 Mb/d in May**. And over twenty years Brazil is the **third-largest absolute increase in world supply — plus 2.9 Mb/d — behind only the US and Canada**. In that scenario, does Brazil become a captive supplier into the US market at a discount, or does it capture the Brent premium? Our crude is medium-light and competes for exactly the Asian barrels that would be short.
>
> **On Wildcard #2 — Russian restrictions.** The fertilizer channel is a bigger deal for Brazil than the report suggests. Brazil consumes about **6.7 million tonnes of nitrogen and produces around 0.7** — we import roughly 90%, much of it Russian. Nitrogen is essentially natural gas in another form, so a Russian export restriction hits Brazilian agriculture through the ammonia route with a lag of one planting season. You mention fertilizers in passing among affected commodities — is there work behind that, or is it a placeholder?"

## 6. Methodology questions (if the conversation goes technical)

- **"You use a demand elasticity of −0.05 to get to $200, and you call it a naïve implementation yourself. What's the range? At −0.08 or −0.10 the price implication drops a lot — how sensitive is the headline number to that assumption?"**
- **"On the 1970s analogy: you adjust for the absence of strategic stocks back then. Do you also adjust for oil intensity? Global oil use per unit of GDP is down roughly 60% since 1979, so getting oil expenditure back to 8% of GDP should require a much higher price than a linear scaling implies."**
- **"How are you defining the Hormuz state — full closure, intermittent, or a risk premium on partial flows? Our balance data is consistent with partial disruption; a sustained 5–10 Mb/d loss would show up much more violently in observable OECD inventories than what we're seeing."**

## 7. Three questions to make sure you ask

1. **"What takes the deficit back to 7–8 Mb/d, when the EIA has it at 2.3 in July and narrowing?"**
2. **"Does the SPR have another 100 million barrels in it — and if not, what absorbs the next shock?"**
3. **"Are Wildcards #1 and #2 really independent, given that low European gas storage forces Europe to bid for the same LNG that a Hormuz disruption takes off the market?"**
